Nash’s theorem via Günther’s trick
Anton Petrunin

Abstract
We present Günther’s approach to Nash’s embedding theorem
with an emphasis on accessibility.

Riemannian geometry has two fundamental theorems. One is about
the existence and uniqueness of the Levi-Civita connection, and the
other is a result of John Nash [11], which states that any Riemannian
manifold is isometric to a smooth submanifold of Euclidean space. In
other words, Riemannian manifolds can be defined as submanifolds of
Euclidean space.
Nash’s original proof is based on a delicate iteration scheme with
smoothing operators to overcome the loss-of-derivatives problem. This
construction led to the Nash–Moser inverse function theorem — a
method, which has found many applications.
Later, Matthias Günther discovered a magic trick [5], which opens
a way with no loss of derivatives problem. As a result, the proof can
be based on the standard inverse function theorem in Banach spaces,
which is essentially a contraction argument.
Thanks to Günther’s simplification, I was able to include this
theorem in a differential geometry course, and I hope it is worth
sharing. Except for the inverse function theorem, I mostly follow
Nash’s original proof; I also use several cosmetic tweaks by Deane
Yang [15], Terence Tao [13], and Ralph Howard [7]. Günther’s approach
is also carefully covered in two textbooks: by Michael Taylor [14] and
by Qing Han and Jia-Xing Hong [6].
The reader is assumed to be familiar with smooth manifolds (charts,
atlases, coverings, partitions of unity, and tensor fields). I use other
results, including Schauder’s estimates and the Whitney embedding
theorem, but they may be treated as black boxes. I would like to
thank Mikhael Gromov, Fedor Nazarov, and Deane Yang for their
help.

We formulat the embedding problem is given in Section 1. Section
2, introduces Nash’s twist and solve an approximate version of the
embedding problem. Section 3 consists of several steps that reduces
the main theorem to a perturbation problem of a metric on the torus.
In Section 5, we introduce free maps and formulate Nash’s lemma and
Günther’s lemma. In the following section we use these lammas to
solve the perturbation problem and therefore the embedding problem.
The final two sections prove the lemmas.

Formulation

Induced metric. Given a smooth n-dimensional manifold Ω, we denote
by TΩ and Tp Ω its tangent bundle and the tangent space at a point p ∈ Ω,
respectively.
Recall that a metric tensor, say g, on Ω is a smooth field of symmetric bilinear
forms on TΩ. Here and further, smooth means C ∞.

Let Ω̄ be another smooth manifold, and let w : Ω → Ω̄ be a smooth map.
Recall that the derivative of w in the direction of a tangent vector x ∈ Tp Ω is
denoted by xw, and xw = dw(x); that is, the derivative of w in the direction x is
the differential dw : TΩ → TΩ̄ of w, evaluated at x. Note that xw ∈ Tw(p) Ω̄.
Suppose that Ω̄ is equipped with a metric tensor ḡ. Then the metric tensor g
on Ω defined by
g(x, y) = ḡ(xw, yw)
is called the induced metric tensor, or the pullback of ḡ to Ω. This relation can
also be written as g = w∗ ḡ. Equivalently, we may say that w : (Ω, g) → (Ω̄, ḡ) is
an isometric.
A metric tensor g is called Riemannian if it is positive-definite; that is, g(x, x) >
0 for any nonzero tangent vector x. A smooth manifold equipped with a Riemannian
metric is called a Riemannian manifold.
Notice that if g = w∗ ḡ is a Riemannian metric, then w must be regular; that
is, the differential dp w has rank n at every point p ∈ Ω. In particular, w is an
immersion.

We consider the space Rd equipped with the metric tensor defined by the
standard scalar product:
ḡ(x, y) := ⟨x, y⟩ = x1 ·y1 + · · · + xd ·yd,
where xi and yi denote the coordinates of vectors x, y ∈ Rd.
 Note that ḡ is
Riemannian.
1.1. Main theorem. Any n-dimensional Riemannian manifold (Ω, g) admits a
smooth isometric embedding into a Euclidean space Rd for some d. Moreover, d
can be bounded in terms of n.
We give a complete proof only for compact manifolds and indicate reduction
of the theorem to the compact case; see Step 4 in the next section.

Torus. Let us denote by Tn = Rn /Zn the n-dimensional torus.
The torus is a smooth manifold; it comes with global cyclic coordinates, which

 A metric tensor on Tn can

Perturbation. The next theorem will be proved in the following sections;
note that this is a very special case of the main theorem.
3.1. Theorem. There exists a Riemannian metric g0 on Tn such that any metric
g that is sufficiently close to g0 in the C ∞ topology, is induced by a smooth map
Tn → Rd for some d depending only on n.
Now we will reduce the main theorem (1.1) to this restricted version. The
reduction proceeds in several steps.

Step 1. Let us get an isometric immersion for any metric on the torus; namely,
➊ given a Riemannian metric g on Tn, there exists an isometric immersion
(Tn, g) ↬ Rd for some d depending only on n.
Indeed, let g0 be the metric from 3.1. After rescaling if necessary, we may
assume that g0 < g; that is, g0 (x, x) < g(x, x) for any nonzero tangent vector x.
In particular, the difference g1 = g − g0 is a Riemannian metric.
By 2.2, there exists a metric tensor h such that for any t > 0, there is a smooth
map w1 : Tn → Rd1 that induces g1 + t·h.
By 3.1, for any small t > 0, there is a smooth map w0 : Tn → Rd0 that induces
the metric g0 − t·h. Therefore, w0 ⊕ w1 induces
g = g0 − t·h + g1 + t·h.

➋ given a Riemannian metric g on Tn, there is an isometric embedding (Tn, g),→
Rd for some d depending only on n.

Free Maps. Recall that a smooth map f : Tn → Rd is regular if its differential

df has rank n at every point. In other words, for any point p ∈ Tn, the first-order
partial derivatives ∂1 f,...
